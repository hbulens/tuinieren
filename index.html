Your climate in Viñuela

Viñuela sits at roughly 200–300 m, inland from Torre del Mar, by the reservoir. The climate is subtropical Mediterranean: winters are your second growing season, and summer is the hard one. If you learn one thing, learn that. Gardening here runs on a different calendar from northern Europe.

What this means for you

  • Plant lettuce, herbs, broad beans and garlic in autumn (Oct–Nov). They grow through the mild winter with free rainwater.
  • Plant tomatoes, basil and squash in spring (Mar–May), so they're well rooted before the heat.
  • Potatoes get two seasons: plant in Jan–Feb (harvest May–Jun) or in Aug–Sep (harvest Nov–Dec).
  • July and August are for survival, not planting. Water at dawn, mulch, and shade.

Local things to watch out for

  • Water is scarce. The Axarquía has had serious droughts and the Viñuela reservoir has run very low. Drip irrigation and mulch aren't optional extras here.
  • Wild boar (jabalí) and rabbits raid gardens in the campo. A sturdy fence, dug in a little, protects months of work.
  • Snails and slugs come out in force after the first autumn rains.
  • Soil on the hillsides is often stony slate (pizarra) or heavy clay, usually alkaline. Feed it well with compost or manure.

Beginner basics

Six habits that matter more than anything else. Get these right and most crops will forgive your other mistakes.

1. Start small

One raised bed (about 1.2 × 2.4 m) plus a few large pots is plenty for year one. A small, well-watered garden beats a big neglected one. You can always expand next autumn.

2. Pick the right spot

Vegetables need at least 6 hours of direct sun. In summer, though, a spot that gets shade after about 4 pm is ideal. Put it close to a tap: you'll be watering every day in July.

3. Feed the soil

Mix in a generous layer (5–10 cm) of compost or well-rotted manure (estiércol) before each season. On stony or clay ground, a raised bed filled with good soil saves a lot of frustration.

4. Drip irrigation + timer

A simple drip kit with a tap timer (riego por goteo + programador) from any garden centre is the best money you'll spend. Water at dawn: deeply and less often is better than a daily sprinkle.

5. Mulch everything

Cover the bare soil around plants with 5–10 cm of straw (paja), dry leaves or woodchip. The soil stays cool and moist, weeds stay down, and you can cut watering by up to half.

6. Buy plants, not seeds (at first)

Young plants (plantones) from a local vivero or village market are cheap and nearly guarantee success. Try seeds for the easy ones (butternut, coriander, broad beans) and graduate to more later.

Growing in pots

No good ground, or just a terrace? Almost everything on this page grows in containers. In the Viñuela summer, pots dry out and heat up much faster than a bed, so the size and material of the pot matter a lot.

Five rules for pots in a hot climate

  1. Use bagged potting soil (sustrato), never garden soil. Garden soil, especially local clay, sets like brick in a pot and drowns roots.
  2. Bigger is better. Small pots can dry out in a single July afternoon. Go for at least 20 litres for tomatoes.
  3. Avoid dark plastic in full sun. It heats up and cooks the roots. Terracotta, wood, light-coloured or fabric pots stay cooler. Terracotta and fabric do dry out faster, though.
  4. Mulch the top with 3–5 cm of straw, bark or gravel, just like in a bed.
  5. Water until it runs out of the bottom, then wait until the top 2–3 cm feel dry. In a summer heatwave, big pots may need water every day, and small ones twice a day.

Every pot needs drainage holes. Never leave a pot standing in a saucer full of water.

Example first bed (1.2 × 2.4 m)

The same bed works all year: winter crops in autumn, summer crops in spring. Rotate: don't put tomatoes or potatoes in the same spot two years in a row (they're related and share diseases).
